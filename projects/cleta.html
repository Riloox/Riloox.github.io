Cleta

A bike share for a campus 3 km outside Fray Bentos. Android app, admin panel, and smart locks that open from the phone over Bluetooth. My final degree project, with Gabriel De León and Mikaella Mateos.

Why it exists

The UTEC campus in Fray Bentos sits about 3 km from the center of town. The bus costs 60 pesos each way, a taxi 150 to 200, and the walk is a walk. Students without a car either pay for a short trip or skip it. The university already lent bikes through a program called EcoMovilidad, one bike at a time, by hand, with a person keeping the list.

Cleta was the version that keeps its own list. A student taps a bike on a map, scans the QR on the frame, and the phone opens the lock over Bluetooth. The loan starts there, counts minutes and ends when the bike comes back to a station, with a photo of where it was left.

The system has three parts. An Android app in React Native, where you sign in with a UTEC Google account, see bikes and stations on a map, open a lock, follow the active loan and report incidents. A web panel in React and TypeScript, where staff manage bikes, stations, locks and users, watch loans in progress, log maintenance and pull reports. And the API in Express and PostgreSQL that holds the rules: one active loan per user, a weekly quota of minutes, an evidence photo at the end.

We were three people and worked eight two-week sprints, sixteen weeks in total, with the final presentation on 22 November 2025. I wrote most of the backend, 426 of its commits: the API, the Prisma schema and migrations, Google OAuth with revocable JWTs, the role checks and the lock service.

What was actually hard

The lock, by a wide margin. Allegion lent us two AXA eRL ring locks and a year of KeySafe Cloud. The NDA took the university from mid June to late August to sign, so the hardware arrived with the calendar already half spent, and the lock side of it has no public documentation at all.

What we had was an API key and a lock on a desk. The KeySafe Cloud API issues an ekey and a passkey for a slot, 16 passkeys per call, valid for 24 hours.

Then the phone has to write them into a GATT characteristic by hand: split the ekey on dashes, encode each part as base64, write the parts in order, then write the first segment of the passkey. The lock answers on a notify characteristic with two bytes. 0,0 means open. 8,0 means the lever is free. Write the parts in the wrong order, skip the segmentation, or send a passkey that was already used, and nothing happens. No error code, no rejection, just a lock that does not open. We found the sequence by reading bytes and trying again, which is a polite way of saying trial and error against a piece of hardware that gives no feedback.

The second problem was time. Waiting on the NDA pushed every hardware test into the last sprints. We used the wait to build the API and the panel ahead of schedule, and the integration still landed late, with the defense date fixed. Every bug found in those weeks had a deadline attached to it.

The third problem was the set of small rules that decide whether the system is fair. A weekly quota of minutes, 120 by default, reset by a scheduled job on Sunday at midnight. A push notification 20 minutes before the quota runs out. One loan at a time per user. Each of those is a sentence in a document and a state machine in the code, and the state machine is where the bugs live.

What I'd change

I would write the lock protocol down the day it worked, in one file, in plain words: part order, byte order, what 0,0 and 8,0 mean, which passkey was spent. It lived in a chat thread and in code comments, and we re-derived parts of it more than once. A page of notes would have saved days.

I would start from a bare React Native build instead of Expo's managed workflow, now that I know the lock is a native BLE library. The stack fought the build more than it fought the hardware, and that was not in the plan.

And I would put tests around the lock and loan code in sprint one. On 17 November, five days before the defense, an automated review of the backend found no test suite and business logic sitting inside the repositories. Both were true. We added Jest after that and it caught real things, but the two places where a mistake costs a bike were the ones we changed every day without a test.
